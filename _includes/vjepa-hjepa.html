06

H-JEPA: a hierarchy of action-conditioned JEPAs

V-JEPA 2-AC predicts one frame ahead and plans one step ahead; pick-and-place works only because a person supplies the intermediate goal images. H-JEPA names the two reasons a single-level JEPA world model struggles further out. Rolling one fine-grained predictor over a long horizon compounds its error and grows the action search space. And one latent space has to serve both short-range dynamics and goal matching, although a goal such as "reach that room" is more abstract than the full state that matches it. Its answer is a stack of JEPAs, each predicting farther ahead in a latent space of its own, trained jointly from pixels. H-JEPA §1

At a glance
Levels
Up to four in simulation, two on DROID. Level 1 is LeWM: a ViT-Tiny encoder read through its [CLS] token and an MLP projector with BatchNorm, and a causal transformer predictor (4 layers, 8 heads, width 192, feed-forward 1024) conditioned on actions through adaptive LayerNorm. §3, App. C.1
Upper levels
A two-layer MLP encoder (hidden 384) applied to one lower-level latent, a one-layer transformer that pools the intervening lower-level action embeddings into a 4-D macro-action, and a causal transformer predictor. Stride sℓ = 2 and window wℓ = 1 at every upper level, so each step up doubles the time a prediction spans. App. C.1, Table 4
Time base
A level-1 step is 5 environment steps with the intervening actions concatenated; a level-ℓ step is 5·2ℓ−1 environment steps. App. C.1
Training
All levels from scratch in one run, upper-level losses backpropagating into the encoders below. No EMA target, no stop-gradient, no pretrained weights. AdamW, weight decay 1e-3, 1% warm-up then cosine decay, clipping at 1.0, bfloat16; peak learning rate 5e-5 at level 1 and 1e-5 above; 5 to 9 epochs; seeds 42, 43, 44; H200 GPUs. Training FLOPs are not matched across depths. App. C.1
Data
1.0M transitions (FourRoom), 10.05M (AntMaze explore plus stitch), the Push-T expert set, the OGBench Cube scripted set; a re-encoded copy of DROID for §07. App. B, C.1, J.4

Building the hierarchy

Higher levels are built from the latents of the level below. With stride sℓ (how far each window advances) and window wℓ (how far it spans), and a:b meaning a, …, b−1:

z(ℓ)t = E(ℓ)( z(ℓ−1)t·sℓ : t·sℓ+wℓ ),   a(ℓ)t = A(ℓ)( a(ℓ−1)t·sℓ+wℓ−1 : (t+1)·sℓ+wℓ−1 )   // Eq. 1

The state encoder pools a w-step window into one abstract state; the action encoder pools the s action embeddings that carry the system from that state to the next upper-level state. Each level is then an ordinary action-conditioned JEPA in its own space, trained by one-step teacher forcing over a context of cℓ states, with SIGReg on the batch of embeddings:

L(ℓ) = (1/cℓ) Στ=1..cℓ ‖ F(ℓ)(z(ℓ)1:τ, a(ℓ)1:τ) − z(ℓ)τ+1 ‖²2 + λℓ·SIGReg(Z(ℓ)),   L = Σℓ L(ℓ)   // Eqs. 2 and 3

Eq. 2 prints the context as z<τ; the text describes a causal predictor that sees the cℓ preceding states and their actions, which is what the line above writes. SIGReg weight λ is shared by all levels of a model: 0.18 on FourRoom, 0.09 elsewhere, with M = 1024 random projections. §2.1, App. C.1, Table 4

Change the depth, stride and window below. The page recomputes how many level-1 frames one training sample needs and checks the result against the paper's Table 3.

What each level keeps: selective abstraction

Two-layer MLP probes on frozen latents, reported as variance-normalised MSE (1 is the marginal mean, 0 is exact recovery), three seeds. In four-level models on Visual AntMaze the ant's joint configuration becomes less recoverable with each level while its position stays recoverable; on FourRoomDistractors the independently moving distractor fades while the agent stays. §3, Fig. 4 The effect tracks the data. Measured as the ratio of spectral centroids of the fastest and slowest state components, environments with a frequency gap below about 2× show little abstraction; AntMaze, Humanoid and FourRoom show it, and the manipulation datasets, Push-T and Cube, have small gaps and do not. §3, Fig. 5, App. F

The mechanism the paper offers is the joint training itself: prediction-error gradients flow into every encoder, so each level keeps what it can predict at its own stride and drops what it cannot. That is a statement about what the objective rewards, demonstrated by probes in three environments, not a guarantee; in tabletop manipulation, where object and gripper move on similar timescales, the hierarchy decomposes time but does not abstract state. Inference

Why diverse scenes need an inverse-dynamics term

On DROID the scene, lighting, camera pose and objects change every episode. Within an episode the background is the most predictable part of the signal, so a purely predictive objective can keep the scene identity and drop the arm. The paper calls this slow-feature collapse and measures it as the within-episode share of latent variance, χ(E) = Eb[tr Vart(zt | b)] / tr Varb,t(zt), which is 0 at exact collapse. Latent variance is 75% between episodes on DROID against 28% on OGBench Cube. §4.3, App. L.1, L.2

Proposition 1. If R is any functional of the marginal law of a single embedding, zero at some law μ0, and the background law is atomless, then an encoder E = Q∘U∘b (reading the background only) and the identity predictor reach Lpred = 0 and R = 0 together. VICReg-style variance and covariance terms, whitening, entropy penalties and SIGReg all look at the marginal law only. A barrier must act on the joint law of z across time.

Theorem 1. For any measurable E, the irreducible prediction risk ℰ(E) = E‖zt+1 − E[zt+1 | zt, at]‖² satisfies ℰ(E) ≥ ν(E) ≥ 0, where ν is the next-embedding variance that survives knowing the full latent state and the action; ℰ = 0 exactly when zt+1 is a measurable function of (zt, at). Reading only the background gives ν = 0 with the identity as predictor. In practice training picked that optimum: action sensitivity fell below 0.05 in all fifteen seeds of the SIGReg ladder.

Proposition 2. At exact collapse, a grouping that puts several timesteps of one sequence into the same normality test raises SIGReg above its null value by Δ = λ(T − 1)κ; a grouping with time in the group axis, which the headline DROID runs use, adds nothing. The ladder runs that do carry Δ all collapse anyway, later at larger λ, with χ between 0.008 and 0.016 past epoch 50.

Proposition 3. With the IDM loss Lidm = (1/(T−1)) Σt (1/da) ‖I(zt, zt+1) − sg[at]‖² (Eq. 8), a collapsed encoder pays at least (1/da) times the action variance not explained by episode identity. With unit-variance actions that floor is 1, so at weight γ = 50 collapse costs 50, against a SIGReg barrier of zero for the grouping used. It needs action labels, and it never penalises keeping distractors.

Read across the family. V-JEPA 2-AC never meets this problem: its encoder is frozen after pretraining on a million hours of varied video, so nothing can collapse, but nothing shapes it toward the arm either, and App. B.4's camera-dependent action error is one symptom. H-JEPA trains a small encoder end to end and must therefore add an explicit, action-supervised barrier. H-JEPA: planning top-down, results and ablations

The planner

Encode the current and goal observations at every level, giving z(ℓ)0 and g(ℓ). The top level optimises macro-actions so that its final predicted state lands on its own goal latent:

a(L),⋆0:HL−1 = argmin ‖ ẑ(L)HL − g(L) ‖²2,   (ẑ1, …, ẑH) = Roll(L)(z0, a0:H−1)   // Eq. 4

Each lower level then plans toward the subgoals the level above predicted. It rolls its own model forward, lifts the rollout into the upper space through the upper encoder, and matches K of the upper predictions, the last one weighted 1 and the intermediate ones β:

z̃(ℓ+1)i = E(ℓ+1)(ẑ(ℓ)i·s : i·s+w),   a(ℓ),⋆ = argmin [ ‖z̃K − ẑ(ℓ+1),⋆K‖² + β Σi<K ‖z̃i − ẑ(ℓ+1),⋆i‖² ]   // Eqs. 5, 6

K = 1 is closed-loop planning, matching only the first subgoal; K = H with β = 0 matches only the terminal prediction; β > 0 also tracks the intermediate ones. Every level optimises by gradient descent through its rollout, not by CEM. §2.2, App. E 

def plan(o, o_goal, levels):                                  # one replanning call
    z, g = [E_l(o) for l], [E_l(o_goal) for l]                   # encode bottom-up, one frame each
    target = g[L]                                                 # top level aims at its goal latent
    for l in L, L-1, ..., 1:
        init = N(0, I) if l == 1 else Gaussian fit to the last 4,000 training macro-actions
        A = S_l candidates from init                              # S_l per level, e.g. (25, 200) on AntMaze
        repeat ≤ 90 AdamW steps, stop when the best cost stalls:
            Z = Roll_l(z[l], A)                                   # context c = 1: latest state only
            cost = ‖Z[-1] − target‖² if l == L                  # level 1 scores its last two states
                   else subgoal cost of Eq. 6 after lifting Z through E_(l+1)
            A ← A − η ∇A cost;  clip upper-level A to the 2nd–98th percentile box
        best = argmin cost;  target = Roll_l(z[l], best)[1]          # first predicted state → subgoal below
    return best level-1 actions

# MPC: execute 2 level-1 blocks (10 environment steps), observe, call plan() again from scratch;
# the top horizon shrinks from H to 1 over the episode, and a level at horizon 1 is skipped.

Why the hierarchy helps: two levers, separated

Hierarchical planning changes two things at once: it splits the search in time, and it scores progress in a more abstract space. The paper isolates the second by keeping level-1 rollouts and planner settings fixed and changing only the space in which the goal distance is measured, projecting both prediction and goal through the upper encoders. On AntMaze every multilevel model gains from at least one projected cost, and the best projected flat planner beats the separately trained LeWM at every depth. Cost maps show why: level-1 distances to an anchor are nearly constant away from it, so a gradient planner gets no signal until it is close, while upper-level distances rise smoothly along corridors. §4.2, Table 1, Fig. 7 The first lever shows in a different measurement: along held-out expert trajectories, the cost of tracking the next subgoal falls monotonically (−Spearman 0.89 to 1.00 for H-JEPA) where the full-horizon goal cost does not (0.48 to 0.75 for the flat level-1 cost). §4.2, Table 10

DROID: real robot video, offline

DROID has no simulator that runs without ray-tracing hardware, so every DROID number is offline and open-loop: one plan covers a fixed 108-frame window (about 7.2 s) centred on the grasp of 16 hand-picked pick-and-place clips with good lighting and visible objects; nothing is executed or replanned. Plans are scored by Fréchet fidelity, how closely the planned end-effector path follows the expert's, relative to not moving at all:

fidelity = ( dF(0, p̃) − dF(p, p̃) ) / dF(0, p̃)   // Eq. 7: 0 = arm still, 100% = expert path, negative = worse than still

p and p̃ are the cumulative xyz paths of the planned and expert delta poses and dF is the discrete Fréchet distance. Each planner seed is averaged over clips, and the median over the 3 × 3 training and planner seeds is reported. The authors calibrate it in simulation: on OGBench Cube no episode succeeds below a fidelity floor, but clearing the floor does not predict success; on RoboCasa CloseDrawer it ranks graded progress with a positive, moderate within-configuration rank correlation. They therefore read a DROID gap as a direction, not a size. §4.3, App. J.1, J.2

The corpus. Raw DROID would make H.264 decoding, not the GPU, set the epoch time over about 5.5 TB. The authors re-encode every camera view to 256 × 256 with libx264 at CRF 18 and repack each trajectory: 127.6 GB for 74,530 episodes, about 45× smaller and 17.9× faster to decode, with trajectories bitwise identical and a median video PSNR of 33.75 dB. The 16 evaluation clips are removed from training. App. J.4, J.1

What the DROID result does and does not show. It shows that on one real-robot corpus with varying scenes, a learned second level improves the path a planner proposes, at less planner compute, under a metric the authors calibrated themselves. It does not show a robot succeeding more often: no plan was executed, the 16 clips were selected for visibility, the baselines with frozen encoders ran at a different frame rate on 15 clips, and the paper's own stated next step is closed-loop control on a physical robot. Inference §6

Limitations, stated and inferred

  • Fixed strides. Upper levels advance by a fixed stride; variable-duration segments are named as future work. §6
  • Action labels are required on diverse scenes. The inverse-dynamics term is the only Ω(1) barrier against slow-feature collapse; time repulsion alone did not clear the zero-action floor. Action-free video is open. App. J.5, L.6, L.7
  • One exterior camera at a time, no wrist view. The authors expect joint multi-view encoding, wrist included, to matter most for precise manipulation. App. J.5
  • Goals are images. Language-aligned upper latents are a stated direction. §6
  • Deterministic at every level. Each predictor is a point estimate trained with L2, so bimodal outcomes such as a slip average out at every timescale, as they do in V-JEPA 2-AC. Inference
  • Small models, simple scenes. The simulated results use a ViT-Tiny with fixed camera and background; whether the selective abstraction survives at V-JEPA 2 scale, or on a frozen V-JEPA 2 encoder, is untested. Inference
