Using the frozen encoder

After pretraining the encoder is frozen. To measure what it knows, a small attentive probe is trained on top: four transformer blocks whose last one replaces self-attention with cross-attention from a learnable query token q to all the encoder's tokens, then a linear classifier: ŷ = W · softmax(qK⊤/√d) V. Only the probe learns. Under one shared protocol, V-JEPA 2 ViT-g reaches 75.3 on Something-Something v2 at 256 px (77.3 at 384 px) against 49.9 to 55.4 for the best image encoders, because SSv2 is about motion, which a single image cannot show. Part 08

V-JEPA 2-AC: frames, states and actions

The world model learns from other people's robot logs with no rewards and no task labels: a frame, the gripper's pose in that frame, and nothing else. An action is simply how the pose changed between two frames. Post-training data Paper §3.1

The data is 23k trajectories from DROID, a Franka Panda arm with a two-finger gripper teleoperated in many labs, successes and failures alike, seen from the left exterior camera at 256 × 256. Each frame carries a 7-D end-effector state s = (x, y, z, three Euler angles, gripper opening) in the robot's base frame. The action between two frames is

ak=sk+1⊖sk=(pk+1−pk,euler(Rk+1Rk⊤),gk+1−gk) Position difference, the rotation that takes one orientation to the next (as extrinsic xyz Euler angles), gripper difference. From droid.py, poses_to_diffs.

What a "frame" is: the file clock and the wall clock

The paper samples DROID "at 4 frames per second". The loader gets that rate by asking the video file for its frame rate and taking every ⌈rate/4⌉-th frame. DROID's files do not report their capture rate: each holds one frame per 15 Hz control step but is timestamped as if recorded at 60 fps. So the loader takes every fifteenth frame, and consecutive training frames are about one second apart in real time. Code, measured

Part 09

V-JEPA 2-AC: the predictor and how it is trained

The encoder stays frozen. All the learning of dynamics happens in a 300M-parameter predictor that reads the past frames, states and actions, and outputs the next frame's feature map. The action-conditioned predictor Paper §3.1 ac_predictor.py

Each frame is encoded on its own (duplicated into a 2-frame tubelet) into 256 tokens of 1408 dimensions, and LayerNormed: zt ∈ ℝ256×1408. The predictor projects patches to width 1024, embeds the 7-D action and 7-D state with two separate linear maps, and lays each time step out as a block of 258 tokens: [at, st, 256 patches]. Twenty-four transformer blocks (width 1024, 16 heads) run with a block-causal mask, the two conditioning tokens are dropped, and the patch outputs of block t are read as the prediction of frame t + 1.

z (B, T·256, 1408) → linear → (B, T, 256, 1024) a, s (B, T, 7) → two linears → (B, T, 1, 1024) each interleave [at, st, zt] per step → (B, T·258, 1024) # T = 16 → 4,128 tokens 24 blocks width 1024, 16 heads, block-causal mask; patches use 3D-RoPE, a and s only the time axis drop a, s tokens → (B, T·256, 1024) → LN → linear → (B, T·256, 1408) → LN # ẑt+1 for every t at once

Build the block-causal attention mask

Rows are queries, columns are keys. A token may attend to every token in its own time step, including the action of that step, and to all earlier steps; never to later ones. Patches are drawn as a few cells per step to keep the grid readable.

Why block-causal

Inside one step, the patches see the action that is about to be applied, so the prediction of frame t + 1 can depend on at. Across steps, nothing sees the future, so one forward pass yields all T next-frame predictions without leakage. build_action_block_causal_attention_mask

Two losses: teacher forcing and a short rollout

Ltf=1T∑k=1T‖Pφ((at,st,zt)t≤k)−zk+1‖1,Lroll=‖Pφ(a1:T;s1,z1)−zT+1‖1,L=Ltf+Lroll Teacher forcing over T = 15 steps from true latents; the rollout term feeds the model its own prediction for T = 2 steps (Eqs. 2 to 4). The released code averages the per-element absolute error over [ẑ1, ẑ2].

Teacher forcing only ever shows the predictor true past states. A planner, however, feeds it its own predictions. The rollout term exposes the predictor once to its own one-step error. The lab shows why that matters and why the deployed planner never asks for more than one step.

How one-step error compounds

Model the rollout error as ek+1 = ρ·ek + δ: each step adds a fresh error δ, and the model maps the previous error forward with gain ρ, its sensitivity to its own input off the data. Teacher forcing sees only δ.

Read the curve

ek = δ(1 − ρk)/(1 − ρ). With ρ > 1 errors grow geometrically, so a model fine at one step can be useless at eight. The rollout term penalises e2 = δ(1 + ρ), which only a lower ρ can reduce. Part 10

Planning: energy, the cross-entropy method, model-predictive control

To act, encode the current frame and a goal image, imagine the outcome of many candidate actions with the predictor, keep the ones whose imagined future is closest to the goal, refit, repeat. Execute the best first action, look again, and plan again. Planning with V-JEPA 2-AC Paper §3.2

E(a^1:H)=‖P(a^1:H;sk,zk)−zg‖1,a1:H⋆=argmina^1:HE,execute a1⋆, observe, repeat The energy is the mean absolute difference between the predicted feature map H steps ahead and the goal's, over 256 × 1408 numbers. It is a terminal cost, not summed along the way.

The cross-entropy method (CEM) searches that energy without gradients. Keep a Gaussian over action sequences, initialised at zero mean. Each iteration: draw S candidate sequences, roll each through the predictor, keep the K with the lowest energy (the elites), and refit the mean and spread to them. After the last iteration, return the mean. Deployed settings: S = 800, K = 10, 10 iterations, horizon H = 1, each translation axis clipped to ±7.5 cm. The released planner smooths the refit with momentum, μ ← 0.85·μelite + 0.15·μ (likewise σ), and samples 400 over a horizon of 2. Paper App. B.2 mpc_utils.py, world_model_wrapper.py

What is inside

  • Encoder: 12 fixed smooth random fields; z = R·image. Never trained on actions, like V-JEPA 2's encoder.
  • Predictor: z′ = z + W·tanh(U[z; a] + b), a 256-unit random hidden layer whose readout W is fitted by ridge regression on the collected transitions.
  • Planner: CEM on the L1 energy between the predicted latent H steps ahead and the goal latent, with the released code's momentum; MPC executes the first action.

Try

  • Fit with 100 transitions, then 2,000: watch the one-step error and the success rate.
  • Rotate the camera at deployment. Up to about 60° the planner still arrives along a curved path; past 90° it fails. V-JEPA 2-AC shows the same kind of camera sensitivity (Part 11).
  • Run to goal from the default start and watch the shading: the planner follows the energy, the L1 distance in the encoder's space, not the distance in the arena. The straight line crosses a ridge of high energy, so the path bends around it.
  • Set K = S: CEM degenerates to averaging random actions.

What it shows, and what it does not

The full loop of a latent world model, pixels to plan, small enough to run in your browser. It is not V-JEPA 2-AC: the encoder is not learned, the world has no contact, and the predictor is fitted, not trained by gradient descent. Toy
# V-JEPA 2-AC deployment, as pseudocode (§3.2, App. B.2) zg = LN(E(goal image)) loop: zk, sk = LN(E(current frame)), proprioception μ, σ = 0, 1 for each action coordinate over the horizon repeat 10 times: A = 800 samples ~ N(μ, σ²), each translation axis clipped to ±0.075 energyi = mean |P(Ai; sk, zk) − zg| # 256 × 1408 numbers μ, σ = mean and std of the 10 lowest-energy samples execute μ, wait for the arm to finish, observe # pick-and-place: sub-goal 1 for 4 steps, 2 for 10, goal for 4

Why it takes 16 seconds. One energy evaluation at horizon 1 is one forward pass of a 300M predictor over 258 tokens, about 2 · 3×10⁸ · 258 ≈ 1.5×10¹¹ operations. 800 samples × 10 iterations makes 1.2×10¹⁵ per action, about 15 s on an RTX 4090 at half its dense peak, close to the reported 16 s. What V-JEPA 2-AC achieved, and where it stops

Zero-shot pick-and-place on Franka arms in two labs absent from DROID, with the same weights and code. Each cell is 10 trials, the multi-stage task needs sub-goal images from a person, and the camera was placed by hand. Paper Tables 2 and 3, §4 Paper

From a single goal image

HWM (arXiv 2604.03208) ran the test the paper does not: one goal image, a Franka in one lab, 10 start-goal pairs per object × 5 trials. Its flat baseline is the V-JEPA 2-AC architecture retrained on the same data as HWM's low level; the hierarchy adds a long-horizon predictor in the same latent space. HWM Table 1

Drawer rates (7 tasks × 5 trials) are not multiples of 1/35, so no counts or intervals can be recovered for them. π0-FAST-DROID and π0.5-DROID are policies fine-tuned on DROID.

Three limits the evidence shows

  • The camera defines the action frame. For camera positions from about 35° to 85°, the action the model infers for 201 random moves is a rotated copy of the executed one, with the rotation error growing almost linearly in the camera angle, about 1.6 cm mean error on 5 cm moves. Lab 11's camera slider reproduces the mechanism. App. B.4
  • Greedy energy, short horizon. Horizon 1 with a terminal energy cannot plan a task that must first move away from the goal image; that is why pick-and-place needs sub-goals, and why the flat planner scores 0 of 50 in HWM's test. Derived
  • Small things are a small share of the energy. The energy averages 256 × 1408 numbers equally. A cup covers a handful of the 256 patches, so a near-miss grasp differs from the goal in about 1.6% of the terms, while the whole energy landscape in the paper's Fig. 9 spans only about 10% of its level.
