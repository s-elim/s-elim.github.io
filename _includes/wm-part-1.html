Part 01

What a world model is, and why predict in a learned space

A world model maps what the agent has seen and what it is about to do onto what it will see next. Plan with it by asking that question for many candidate actions before moving. The definition used throughout Derived

Formally it is a conditional predictor ŝt+1 = f(s≤t, at). The choice that separates the families is what s is. A video model such as Cosmos or Genie predicts pixels, or a compressed code it can decode back to pixels. A JEPA predicts an embedding produced by an encoder that is trained jointly with the predictor, and never decodes.

The argument for the second choice is a decomposition. For any prediction ŷ of a target y given a context x, the expected squared error splits in two:

E‖y−y^‖2=‖E[y∣x]−y^‖2⏟what a model can fix+tr⁡Cov(y∣x)⏟irreducible The second term is the variance of the target that the context cannot explain.

In pixels, the irreducible term is dominated by things nothing can forecast: leaves, water, sensor noise, specular highlights. A pixel loss still spends gradient on them. In a learned space the encoder is free to drop what it cannot predict, so the loss pays only for what is predictable. The V-JEPA 2 paper makes the same point about "each blade of grass in a field".   • Raise the encoder bandwidth: the feature space starts to keep flicker, and its share climbs back toward the pixel value. Lower it below 6: the encoder blurs away the object too, and the share climbs again. A JEPA encoder is rewarded for keeping what moves and dropping what flickers.

The energy view

LeCun's 2022 position paper frames this as an energy-based model: a scalar that is low when a continuation y fits a context x, with no need to be a normalised probability. A joint-embedding predictive architecture computes that energy in representation space. LeCun 2022

E(x,y,z)=D(Pφ(Eθ(x),z),sg(Eθ¯(y))) Eθ: online encoder. Eθ̄: target encoder. Pφ: predictor. z: what the predictor is told about the target (masked positions, or an action). D: a distance (L1 or L2). sg: stop-gradient.

Every model in this lecture is an instance. In V-JEPA 2, x is the visible part of a clip, y the hidden part and z the positions to fill. In V-JEPA 2-AC, x is the past frames, y the next frame and z the action. In H-JEPA there is one such energy per timescale. Planning then searches over z (actions) to make the energy between the predicted future and a goal low.

The catch

A deterministic predictor trained with L1 converges to the conditional median of the target features. A learned space removes multimodality only if the encoder maps different outcomes close together, which means it discarded the random factor. In manipulation that factor (does the cup slip?) can be the one that decides the task.

Part 02

The family on one map

One move repeated: encode the visible, predict the encoding of the hidden or the future. Five design choices change from model to model. The lineage, I-JEPA to H-JEPA Derived

The five choices: what is hidden (image blocks, video tubes, the next frame, a state several steps ahead), what the prediction is conditioned on (positions, actions, pooled macro-actions), the distance (L2 or L1), how collapse is prevented (a slow teacher, a batch regulariser, an action-regression head), and what the result is used for (frozen features, or a simulator a planner searches). Part 03

From pixels to tokens

A Vision Transformer never sees pixels one by one. It cuts the input into bricks, flattens each brick and projects it to a vector: one token per brick. Patch and tubelet embedding Paper §2.1

For a video x ∈ ℝ3×T×H×W, V-JEPA 2 uses a 3D convolution whose kernel equals its stride, 2 × 16 × 16. Each brick of 2 frames × 16 × 16 pixels × 3 colours (1,536 numbers) becomes one d-dimensional token; d = 1408 for the 1B ViT-g. The number of tokens is

N=Ttp·Hp·Wp,N=8·16·16=2048for 16 frames at 256 px tp = 2 frames per tubelet, p = 16 pixels per side. A 64-frame clip at 384 px gives 32 · 24 · 24 = 18,432 tokens.

Attention compares every token with every other, so its cost grows with N², and a 9× longer sequence costs about 81× more per layer. That is why V-JEPA 2 trains at 16 frames and 256 px and only cools down at 64 frames and 384 px. Paper §2.4, Table 9

Part 04

Inside the encoder: attention and 3D rotary positions

Each of ViT-g's 40 blocks lets every token read every other, weighted by how well their queries and keys match, then transforms each token on its own. The transformer block Paper Table 12

h=x+MHSA(LN(x)),y=h+MLP(LN(h)),Attn(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dh)V ViT-g: width 1408, 40 blocks, 22 heads of dh = 64, MLP width 6144. Q, K, V are linear maps of the normalised tokens.

Attention by itself does not know where a token is. V-JEPA 2 tells it with 3D rotary position embedding (RoPE): each head's 64 dimensions are split into three segments of 20, rotated by the token's time, row and column index respectively, and the last 4 are left alone. Rotating queries and keys by angles proportional to position makes their dot product depend on the difference of positions, so attention sees relative offsets. Paper §2.1 modules.py

(q2j′q2j+1′)=(cospωj−sinpωjsinpωjcospωj)(q2jq2j+1),ωj=10000−j/(D/2),⟨Rpq,Rp′k⟩=⟨q,Rp′−pk⟩ Intended RoPE on one axis segment of size D. p is the token's index along that axis.
Lab 3

Rotary positions, intended and as implemented

Score one query against one key as the key slides away along an axis, starting from two different base positions. Intended RoPE gives the same curve from both starts. The released code tiles its frequencies instead of interleaving them, which its own comment calls a bug kept for compatibility with the trained weights.

-max gap between curves
-norm after rotation

What the code does

rotate_queries_or_keys builds sin and cos for D/2 frequencies, repeats them end to end (ω0…ωD/2−1, ω0…) and applies them to adjacent pairs, so the two coordinates of a pair get different angles and the map is not a rotation. src/models/utils/modules.py @ 204698b

Does it matter?

The model was trained this way, so it learned around it; the measured gap shows only that attention is not purely relative in position, which is a property papers often assume. Derived
Part 05

The JEPA objective: hide, encode, predict, compare

Hide part of the input. Encode what is left. Ask a predictor for the encoding of what was hidden, and compare it with what a slowly updated copy of the encoder says the hidden part looks like. I-JEPA, V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2 Papers

I-JEPA does this on images. From one large context block (85 to 100% of the image) it predicts the representations of four target blocks (15 to 20% each, aspect 0.75 to 1.5), removed from the context, with an L2 loss. V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2 do it on video, with blocks that run through the whole clip in time (tubes) and an L1 loss, which V-JEPA found more stable:

minθ,φ,Δy1|M|∑i∈M‖Pφ(Δy,Eθ(xC))i−sg(LN(Eθ¯(x)))i‖1,θ¯←mθ¯+(1−m)θ C: visible (context) tokens; M: masked tokens; Δy: a learnable mask token placed at each hidden position; LN: LayerNorm on the targets; m = 0.99925 in V-JEPA 2, fixed. Loss averaged over the short-range and long-range masks.

Why tubes: neighbouring frames are almost identical, so a token hidden at time t but visible at t ± 1 could be copied rather than inferred. Masking the whole time extent removes that shortcut. Because the context still contains every frame, this is spatiotemporal inpainting, not extrapolation; the explicit predict-the-next-frame objective appears only in V-JEPA 2-AC. V-JEPA's ablation: random tubes at 90% masking give 51.5 / 46.4 / 55.6 on K400 / SSv2 / IN1K against 72.9 / 67.4 / 72.8 for multiblock. V-JEPA Table 4

Part 06

Why it does not collapse

The loss above has a perfect, useless minimum: an encoder that outputs the same vector for every input predicts itself exactly. Something must keep the representation alive. Representation collapse Derived

If Eθ(x) = c for every x, then the predictor outputs c and the loss is zero. V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2 rely on three asymmetries. A stop-gradient on the target branch, so the target is never pulled toward the prediction. An EMA teacher, θ̄ ← mθ̄ + (1 − m)θ, a lagged copy; at m = 0.99925 it averages roughly the last 1/(1 − m) ≈ 1,333 steps. A predictor, which gives the online branch a degree of freedom the target lacks. Tian, Chen and Ganguli (ICML 2021) analyse why this works for linear networks; SimSiam showed the EMA is not needed if stop-gradient and a predictor are kept. V-JEPA 2 reports no rank or spectrum diagnostic. Paper §2.1 Tian et al. 2021

Lab 5

Train a linear JEPA and watch it collapse, or not

An 8-dimensional input with decaying variances, two noisy views, a 4-dimensional linear encoder W, a linear predictor P, an EMA target. The page trains it live with gradient descent and plots the singular values of W: the size of each direction the representation keeps.

-directions alive
-final loss

What to expect

  • No stop-gradient: every singular value falls to zero within about a thousand steps, and the loss reaches zero.
  • No predictor: a slower collapse.
  • Stop-gradient plus predictor, with or without EMA: no collapse; with the default weight decay all four directions stay.
  • The full recipe at weight decay 0: the weakest direction falls far below the others, in some runs to zero. That is the partial dimensional collapse the literature reports, and weight decay is one of the things holding it off here.

What it shows, and what it does not

The mechanism in the simplest setting where it can be computed: the linear setting of Tian et al. A 1B-parameter ViT is not guaranteed to behave the same, and V-JEPA 2 does not measure it. H-JEPA replaces all three tricks with a regulariser on the batch (Part 13). Toy
