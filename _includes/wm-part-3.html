Part 12

H-JEPA: a stack of world models, one per timescale

Each level is an action-conditioned JEPA in its own latent space. Higher levels look further ahead, in coarser time, over states built from the level below. Zhang, Terver, Rabbat, LeCun, Balestriero, arXiv 2610.06805 H-JEPA §2.1

The problem it addresses: a single-level JEPA world model rolled over a long horizon compounds its error (Lab 10) and searches an action space that grows with every step, and one latent space has to serve both short-range dynamics and goal matching, even when the goal ("reach that room") is more abstract than the state that satisfies it. H-JEPA's answer is a hierarchy trained end to end from pixels. Level 1 encodes each observation, z(1)t = E(1)(ot), and each action block, a(1)t = A(1)(at). Level ℓ > 1 pools the level below with a stride sℓ (how far each window advances) and a window wℓ (how many steps it spans):

zt(ℓ)=E(ℓ)(ztsℓ:tsℓ+wℓ(ℓ−1)),at(ℓ)=A(ℓ)(atsℓ+wℓ−1:(t+1)sℓ+wℓ−1(ℓ−1)) Eq. 1. The state encoder pools a w-step window into one abstract state; the action encoder pools the s action embeddings that move the system to the next upper-level state (a macro-action). All experiments use w = 1.

Every level is then trained as its own JEPA, by one-step teacher forcing over a context of cℓ states, plus a regulariser on its batch of embeddings, and all levels are summed and trained together, with upper-level losses backpropagating into the encoders below:

L(ℓ)=1cℓ∑τ=1cℓ‖F(ℓ)(z1:τ(ℓ),a1:τ(ℓ))−zτ+1(ℓ)‖22+λℓSIGReg(Z(ℓ))+γℓLidm(ℓ),L=∑ℓL(ℓ) Eqs. 2, 3 and 8. No EMA target, no stop-gradient on targets, no pretrained weights. The inverse-dynamics term (γ > 0) is used on DROID only. Eq. 2 prints the context as z<τ; the line above follows the text's causal description.

Simulation models. Level 1 is LeWM: a ViT-Tiny encoder read through its [CLS] token and an MLP projector with BatchNorm, and a causal transformer predictor (4 layers, 8 heads, width 192) conditioned on actions through adaptive LayerNorm; a level-1 step is 5 environment steps. Upper levels: a two-layer MLP encoder on one lower-level latent, a one-layer transformer pooling two action embeddings into a 4-D macro-action, a causal transformer predictor; s = 2 and w = 1, so each level up doubles the time a prediction spans. The arithmetic

The top level needs nL = 4 states; then nℓ−1 = (nℓ − 1)sℓ + wℓ, and a clip spans 5(n1 − 1) + 1 environment steps. To cover K upper-level subgoals, a lower level plans Hℓ = K·sℓ+1 + wℓ+1 − 1 steps. App. C.1, §2.2

Why depth costs data

A clip must lie inside one episode. Four levels need 121-step clips, so short-episode datasets such as Push-T yield only 14% of the flat model's training transitions per epoch, and four-level Push-T fails. Table 3, §4.1

What each level keeps

Probes on the frozen latents of four-level models show selective abstraction: on Visual AntMaze the ant's joint configuration becomes less recoverable at each level up while its position stays recoverable; on FourRoom the randomly moving distractor fades while the agent stays. The effect appears only when the fast and slow factors in the data move at timescales at least about 2× apart (AntMaze, Humanoid, FourRoom), not in Push-T or Cube. The mechanism is the joint training: each encoder gets gradient from a predictor working at its own stride, so it keeps what is predictable at that stride. §3, Figs. 4 and 5

Collapse without a teacher: SIGReg, and why diverse scenes need inverse dynamics

H-JEPA drops the EMA teacher. It keeps embeddings alive by pushing each batch toward an isotropic Gaussian, and on varied robot scenes it must add a head that recovers the action from consecutive states. H-JEPA App. K, L; LeJEPA Papers

SIGReg (Balestriero and LeCun, LeJEPA) tests normality one direction at a time. Project the n embeddings of a batch onto M random unit directions um, and for each projection compare its empirical characteristic function with that of a standard normal. By the Cramér–Wold theorem, matching every one-dimensional projection means matching the whole distribution.

SIGReg(Z)=1M∑m=1MTEP(Zum),TEP(h)=n∫|φ^h(t)−e−t2/2|2w(t)dt,φ^h(t)=1n∑ieithi Eq. 9 with the Epps–Pulley statistic; Gaussian weight w(t). The LeWM implementation evaluates the integral on [0, 3] with 17 trapezoidal knots, using that the integrand is even. On Gaussian data the statistic sits at a floor of about 1; on collapsed data it grows linearly with n.

Slow-feature collapse

On DROID the scene, lighting, camera pose and objects change every episode, and within one episode the background is the most predictable thing in view. A purely predictive encoder can therefore keep the scene identity and drop the moving arm: its latents stay constant within each episode and next-step prediction becomes trivial. The paper calls this slow-feature collapse and measures it by the within-episode share of latent variance, χ, which is 0 at exact collapse. Latent variance is 75% between episodes on DROID against 28% on OGBench Cube. §4.3, App. L.1, L.2

Its key results: no regulariser that looks only at the distribution of single embeddings can prevent it, SIGReg included, because a background-only encoder can have any distribution you like (Proposition 1); the prediction objective actually prefers it, since it has zero irreducible error (Theorem 1); and an inverse-dynamics head, which must recover at from (zt, zt+1), makes collapse cost at least the action variance that episode identity cannot explain (Proposition 3).

Planning top-down, and what it buys

The top level plans macro-actions toward its own goal latent. Its predicted states become the targets of the level below, which plans finer actions to reach them, down to primitive actions. H-JEPA §2.2 Paper

a(L),⋆=argmin‖z^HL(L)−g(L)‖22,a(ℓ),⋆=argmin[‖z~K−z^K(ℓ+1),⋆‖2+β∑i<K‖z~i−z^i(ℓ+1),⋆‖2] Eqs. 4 to 6. z̃i = E(ℓ+1)(lower-level rollout at the i-th upper step): the lower plan is lifted into the upper space and compared with the upper level's predictions. K = 1 is closed-loop (track only the first subgoal); β weights intermediate subgoals.

Unlike V-JEPA 2-AC, every level optimises its actions by gradient descent through its own rollout (AdamW, at most 90 steps, early stopping), not by CEM. Upper levels have no natural action bounds, so their search starts from a Gaussian fitted to the last 4,000 training macro-actions and is clipped to their 2nd–98th percentiles. The agent executes two level-1 blocks (10 environment steps) and replans from scratch. App. E

Two things change at once, and the paper separates them. Temporal decomposition: tracking the next subgoal is an easy, short problem; along expert trajectories the subgoal-tracking cost falls monotonically (−Spearman 0.89 to 1.00) where the full-horizon goal cost does not (0.48 to 0.75). A better space to measure progress: keeping level-1 rollouts fixed and only measuring goal distance through the upper encoders raises success on AntMaze and FourRoom; level-1 distances are nearly flat far from the goal, upper-level distances rise smoothly along corridors. §4.2, Tables 1, 10, Fig. 7

Read it carefully

The DROID result shows a better planned path, not a robot succeeding more often: no plan was executed, the clips were chosen for visibility, and the baselines ran at a different frame rate on a different set. The authors name closed-loop control on a physical robot as their next step.

How this maps onto my work

Agentic latent world models: top-down subgoal refinement is latent lookahead across timescales, and HWM's 0% to 70% is the clearest evidence yet that a manipulation planner needs it. Representation: an upper level that keeps only what it can predict at its stride is close to the outcome-sufficient representations I study, and the per-entity probes are the test I would reuse. Dexterous manipulation: whether a hierarchy separates finger joints from object pose decides whether it helps hands at all. None of this is a result yet.
